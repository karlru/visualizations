<Chapter id="the-problem" title="Five cities, one round trip" scene="five-cities" eyebrow="Chapter 01" length="tall">

Here is the whole problem. A salesman has five cities to visit. He starts in one
city, must visit every other city exactly once, and must come home. The drawing
shows one possible round trip: follow the line from city to city until it closes
back at the start.

This is the **traveling salesman problem**, usually shortened to TSP. A route
like the one on screen is called a **tour**. It is a closed cycle, not an open
path. The edge that returns to the starting city is part of the tour, and it
counts in the tour's length. The length is the sum of all five edges. A shorter
tour means less driving.

The salesman does not care which city he visits first, and he does not care
whether he travels clockwise or anticlockwise. He cares about one thing: among
all the possible round trips, which one is shortest?

<Callout tone="idea" title="The only rule">

Visit every city once, return to the start, and keep the total distance as small
as possible.

</Callout>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="try-every-tour" title="Try every tour" scene="enumerate-tours" eyebrow="Chapter 02" length="tall">

With five cities, the number of possible tours is small enough to write down.
Fix city 1 as the start. The other four cities can be visited in any order, which
gives $4! = 24$ orders. Each round trip appears twice in that list, once in each
direction, so there are only **12 distinct tours**.

That means we can solve the problem the obvious way. List all twelve tours,
measure each one, and keep the shortest. This is called **brute force**: try
everything, then choose the best. The animation checks one tour at a time and
keeps the shortest one it has seen so far in amber.

On this map the shortest tour is **1.617** units long. Brute force proves it is
the shortest because it checked every alternative. No guessing and no shortcuts.
For five cities, that is a perfectly good algorithm.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="too-many-tours" title="Too many tours" scene="factorial-wall" eyebrow="Chapter 03" length="tall">

The trouble starts when the map grows. Adding one city does not add one more
tour; it multiplies the number of tours by the number of cities. The animation
adds cities in stages, and the table shows what happens to the work.

| Cities | Distinct tours | Time at one billion tours per second |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 12 | instant |
| 10 | 181,440 | instant |
| 15 | 4.4 × 10¹⁰ | 44 seconds |
| 20 | 6.1 × 10¹⁶ | about 704 days |
| 30 | 4.4 × 10³⁰ | about 10,000 × the age of the universe |

The count is $(n-1)!/2$ for $n$ cities. The exclamation mark means **factorial**:
multiply every whole number from 1 up to that number. Factorials grow very fast.
At thirty cities, even a computer checking a billion tours every second would not
finish before the Sun burns out.

So brute force is exact, but it does not scale. We need a method that gives up
the promise of the absolute shortest tour and looks for a good one quickly. That
kind of method is called a **heuristic**.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="one-ants-choice" title="One ant, alone" scene="one-ant" eyebrow="Chapter 04" length="tall">

Go back to the five cities. Now forget the salesman for a moment and watch one
ant. It starts at city 1. It can see the city it is standing in, the cities it has
not visited yet, and the distance to each of them. It cannot see the whole map,
the best tour, or what any other ant is doing.

At each step it chooses one of the **allowed** cities: the ones it has not
visited. Closer cities are more likely to be chosen, but the choice is not
certain. The ant samples from the possibilities, so it sometimes takes a longer
edge. That randomness is useful: it lets the ant try routes that a greedy rule
would never explore.

As it walks, the ant leaves a faint chemical trail on the edges it uses. The
trail is called **pheromone**. A single ant can complete a tour this way, but its
first tour is not necessarily the shortest. On this map its first tour is 1.737
units long, while the true shortest is 1.617.

<Callout tone="idea" title="Local, not global">

The ant never plans the whole route. It makes one small decision at a time, using
only what is nearby.

</Callout>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="leaving-a-trace" title="Leaving a trail" scene="deposit" eyebrow="Chapter 05" length="tall">

A completed tour is information, and the ant writes it into the world. After it
returns home, it adds pheromone to every edge it used. The amount is $Q/L$, where
$L$ is the length of that ant's tour and $Q$ is a fixed constant, set to 1 here.
A tour of length 2 adds 0.5 to each of its edges; a tour of length 4 adds 0.25.
Within one tour every edge gets the same amount, because the whole route is
judged by its total length.

A shorter tour therefore leaves a stronger recommendation on all of its edges.
When another ant comes along, it is more likely to choose a strongly marked
edge. That ant then completes a tour of its own and adds another layer of
pheromone. The trail shapes the next choice, and the next choice reshapes the
trail. This is **positive feedback**.

Because the ants communicate by changing a shared environment rather than by
talking to each other, biologists call it **stigmergy**. The trail is the
message, and every ant both reads it and writes it.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="forgetting" title="Trails fade" scene="evaporation" eyebrow="Chapter 06" length="tall">

If pheromone only ever accumulated, the first trail would win forever. A lucky
early route could keep attracting ants even after a better route appeared. The
colony needs a way to forget.

After each round, every edge loses a fraction of its pheromone. If the
evaporation rate is $\rho$, then a fraction $(1-\rho)$ remains before the new
deposits are added. With $\rho = 0.5$, half of each trail survives each round. A
trail that no ant used shrinks. A trail that keeps being used is renewed and can
grow again.

Evaporation gives the search a horizon: recent evidence matters more than old
evidence. At $\rho = 0$ nothing fades, and stale paths persist. At $\rho = 1$ the
map is erased before every deposit, so the colony loses most of its shared
memory. The balanced setting is somewhere in between.

<Callout tone="warning" title="Forgetting is a feature">

Without evaporation, the colony would stop responding to what it learns later.
The point is not to remember everything; it is to remember what keeps proving
useful.

</Callout>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="two-ants" title="A second ant" scene="two-ants" eyebrow="Chapter 07" length="tall">

Now add a second ant. The first ant has already left a trail. The second ant
starts its own tour and sees that trail on the edges around it. It may follow a
strong edge, or it may take a different one. Either way, it completes a tour and
deposits pheromone of its own.

The two tours are not independent any more. The first ant changed the world, and
the second ant responds to the change. If the first tour was short, its edges are
more attractive, so the second ant is more likely to reuse them. If the second
ant finds an even shorter route, its deposit can start to pull the colony in a
new direction.

On this five-city map, two ants find the shortest tour after two rounds. That is
not a guarantee; it is what happened with this map and this seed. The important
part is the loop: a tour leaves a trail, and the trail changes the next tour.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="emergence" title="A few more ants" scene="colony" eyebrow="Chapter 08" length="tall">

Add a few more ants, four in total. Each one builds a tour, each one deposits
pheromone, and the shared trail becomes easier to read. Edges that appear in
several short tours grow stronger. Edges that only appeared in long tours fade.

The colony now reaches the same **1.617** tour that brute force found in chapter
2. No ant planned that route. Each ant made a local choice, and the repeated
choices left a trail that made the good route easier for the next ant to find.
That is **emergence**: a useful global pattern produced by simple local rules.

Two numbers describe the run. The **best length** is the shortest tour found
since the start. It is a record, so it can only stay the same or improve. The
**mean length** is the average of the current round's tours, and it can move up
and down as the colony explores. A bad round can raise the mean while the record
holds.

<Callout tone="try" title="Watch the record, not the average">

Best length never rises. The mean is a snapshot of the current ants, so it is
allowed to wander. The gap between them tells you how much the colony is still
exploring.

</Callout>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="the-probability-rule" title="The rule behind the choice" scene="probability-rule" eyebrow="Chapter 09" length="normal">

Now we can write down the rule the ant has been following. At city $i$, the
probability of moving to an allowed city $j$ is proportional to pheromone raised
to $\alpha$ times inverse distance raised to $\beta$:

<Math
  id="formula-transition"
  label="Transition probability"
  caption="The chance of choosing city j from city i is the pheromone weight multiplied by the inverse-distance weight, divided by the sum of that product over every city l that is still allowed."
  tex={ACO_TRANSITION_TEX}
/>

Here $\tau_{ij}$ is the pheromone on the edge, and $\eta_{ij} = 1/d_{ij}$ is the
inverse distance. The numerator is a weight; the denominator adds the weights of
every allowed city, which makes the probabilities sum to one. The Greek letter
$\tau$ (tau) is the pheromone, and $\eta$ (eta) is the distance term.

The exponents set the balance. A larger $\alpha$ makes differences in pheromone
matter more. A larger $\beta$ makes differences in distance matter more. Neither
is an on/off switch. At $\alpha = 1$ and $\beta = 2$, a slightly shorter edge and
a slightly stronger trail both raise a candidate's weight. The ant samples from
the result; it does not simply take the maximum.

With $\beta = 0$, the distance term becomes 1 for every candidate, so only
pheromone remains. With $\alpha = 0$, pheromone is ignored and the weight is
$1/d^{\beta}$. That is distance-driven sampling, not the nearest-neighbour rule:
the closest city is the most likely, but it is not certain.

<Predict question="If β = 0 and α stays at 1, can the colony still find short tours?">

<p>
  At first, only by accident. With no distance signal, the first tours are
  effectively random, and the pheromone they leave records whichever routes
  happened to be short. Later ants follow those trails, so the colony can drift
  toward an early lucky route. But it has no reliable gradient toward the nearest
  city, and on the default map it ends up worse than the balanced preset.
  Turning β back on restores the distance signal.
</p>

</Predict>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="the-update-rule" title="The rule behind the trail" scene="update-rule" eyebrow="Chapter 10" length="normal">

The choice rule needs pheromone. The update rule says where it comes from. After
an ant finishes a tour of length $L_k$, it deposits a fixed amount on every edge
it used:

<Math
  id="formula-deposit"
  label="Deposit for one ant"
  caption="Ant k adds Q divided by the length of its tour to every edge it used, and nothing to every other edge. Q is fixed at 1 in this release."
  tex={ACO_DEPOSIT_TEX}
/>

Then every edge is updated. Evaporation happens first, and the deposits are
added after it:

<Math
  id="formula-update"
  label="Evaporation and update"
  caption="Every edge first keeps one minus rho of its pheromone, then receives the sum of the deposits from all ants that used it. Evaporation happens before deposit."
  tex={ACO_UPDATE_TEX}
/>

The order matters. Evaporating first means an edge keeps only a fraction of its
old value before the new evidence arrives. A trail that no ant used shrinks; a
trail that keeps being used is renewed. The deposit term is larger for shorter
tours, because $Q/L_k$ grows as $L_k$ falls.

Positive feedback and evaporation pull in opposite directions. Feedback makes
good routes easier to find again; evaporation stops an early route from locking
in forever. A colony that balances the two can keep improving without forgetting
everything it has learned.

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="tuning-the-colony" title="Tuning the colony" scene="tuning" eyebrow="Chapter 11" length="normal">

The three parameters control that balance. $\alpha$ sets how strongly pheromone
matters, $\beta$ sets how strongly distance matters, and $\rho$ sets how quickly
old trails fade. The four presets are named combinations of those values:

<dl class="preset-list">
  <div class="preset-list__item">
    <dt>Balanced</dt>
    <dd><span class="data">α = 1, β = 2, ρ = 0.5</span> — pheromone and distance both matter.</dd>
  </div>
  <div class="preset-list__item">
    <dt>Greedy</dt>
    <dd><span class="data">α = 0.5, β = 8, ρ = 0.5</span> — distance dominates, so the colony becomes short-sighted.</dd>
  </div>
  <div class="preset-list__item">
    <dt>Stagnant</dt>
    <dd><span class="data">α = 5, β = 1, ρ = 0.1</span> — pheromone dominates and early paths lock in.</dd>
  </div>
  <div class="preset-list__item">
    <dt>Forgetful</dt>
    <dd><span class="data">α = 1, β = 2, ρ = 0.95</span> — strong evaporation keeps resetting the trails.</dd>
  </div>
</dl>

The tension is **exploration** versus **exploitation**. Exploration tries edges
that have not proved themselves; exploitation returns to trails that already
look good. Greedy is short-sighted: with $\beta = 8$ the nearest edges dominate,
so tours look locally sensible and can miss a better global arrangement.
Stagnant is the opposite: with $\alpha = 5$ and $\rho = 0.1$, an early trail is
almost impossible to overturn, so the colony can lock in a mediocre route.
Forgetful keeps moving by discarding most of the trail each round, at the cost of
forgetting good structure. Balanced is the readable default.

The same method also works on larger maps. The figure shows the best tour found
on the default thirty-city map after forty rounds. Its length is 4.91 in the
map's normalized units, where the whole unit square spans one unit. That is one
run with one seed, not a benchmark.

<Chapter id="when-it-fails" title="When it fails" scene="limits" eyebrow="Chapter 12" length="normal">

Ant System is a heuristic. It has no optimality guarantee, and the tour it finds
can be improved by a different run, a different seed, or a different parameter
set.

<h3>Limitations</h3>

Local optima are the first limit. The record can plateau. A plateau is not proof
of optimality; it means this colony, on this map, stopped finding improvements.
A later variant or a different seed might still find a shorter tour.

Cost grows quickly. Each round builds one tour per ant, and each construction
step compares up to $n$ candidates, so the work is roughly proportional to
ants × $n^2$. More ants buy more samples per round, not a better guarantee.

Parameter sensitivity is the third limit. The same map can end at different
lengths depending on $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\rho$, and the seed changes the city
layout and the sampled choices. A setting that works on one instance is not
automatically a good default for another.

The variant matters too. This story implements **Ant System**, the 1996
formulation by Dorigo, Maniezzo, and Colorni. Ant Colony System and Max–Min Ant System
are later refinements: they change the transition and update rules, for example
with stronger exploitation or explicit bounds on pheromone. They are not
implemented here.

<h3>Applications</h3>

Where a good-enough tour beats a guaranteed optimum, ant colony methods are used
in practice. Vehicle routing plans deliveries under capacity and time-window
constraints. Network design lays out links and cable across a set of sites.
Scheduling sequences jobs whose setup costs depend on the order. Dynamic
combinatorial problems, where the instance changes while the solution is in use,
suit the incremental nature of a pheromone trail: the colony can adapt an
existing trail instead of starting over. The TSPLIB benchmark library is the
standard way to compare traveling-salesman implementations on shared instances.

<Callout tone="warning" title="A heuristic is not a guarantee">

The best tour is the best one this run found. Nothing in the method proves that a
shorter tour does not exist.

</Callout>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="the-lab" title="The lab" scene="lab" eyebrow="Chapter 13" length="normal">

The lab is where you change the experiment. It starts paused at round zero, with
the default thirty-city map. Press **Start simulation** to run the colony, or
place and move cities first to build your own map.

Choose the number of ants, the pheromone weight $\alpha$ (0–5), the distance
weight $\beta$ (0–10), and the evaporation rate $\rho$ (0–1). The four presets
are shortcuts to the combinations in the previous chapter. Speed (0.5×, 1×, 2×,
4×) changes how fast rounds arrive, not what a round does. The seed reproduces a
run: the same seed produces the same map and the same sampled choices.

The stage offers play, pause, step, restart, and share. It also offers layer
toggles, camera framing, and map editing. Drag a city handle or focus it and use
the arrow keys. Add city, Remove selected, and Reset map keep the instance
between 2 and 150 cities. Every map edit pauses the run, rebuilds distances, and
restarts at iteration 0.

A share link records the seed, the structural parameters, the speed, and the
chapter you are reading; it restarts at step 0 and restores a seeded map rather
than your local city positions. City-map edits are local to your session: they
are not encoded in a share link. Side-by-side comparison of two parameter sets is
deferred for this release; run one configuration at a time, or share two links
and open them side by side.

<Callout tone="try" title="Start simple">

Try one ant on the default map first. Then raise the ant count and watch the
trails concentrate. Change one parameter at a time so you can tell what caused
the difference.

</Callout>

</Chapter>

<section id="glossary" class="story-appendix prose" aria-labelledby="glossary-title">

<h2 id="glossary-title">Glossary</h2>

<dl class="glossary">
  <dt>Ant (agent)</dt>
  <dd>One member of the colony. It sees only its current city, its unvisited set, and the local pheromone; it constructs one complete tour per round.</dd>
  <dt>Brute force</dt>
  <dd>A method that checks every candidate and keeps the best. It is exact, but the number of candidates can grow beyond what any computer can check.</dd>
  <dt>Candidate tour</dt>
  <dd>One complete round trip that the search is considering or has already checked.</dd>
  <dt>Pheromone</dt>
  <dd>A non-negative value on each edge, written by ants and reduced by evaporation. It is the colony's shared memory and the main input to the transition rule.</dd>
  <dt>Stigmergy</dt>
  <dd>Indirect communication through a shared environment. An ant changes the trail; later ants respond to the changed trail without meeting the first ant.</dd>
  <dt>Tour</dt>
  <dd>A cycle that visits every city exactly once. The edge from the last city back to the first is part of the tour and part of its length.</dd>
  <dt>Traveling salesman problem (TSP)</dt>
  <dd>The problem of finding the shortest tour through a set of cities with known distances. The symmetric version has the same distance in both directions.</dd>
  <dt>Heuristic</dt>
  <dd>A method that aims for a good solution without guaranteeing the best one. Ant System is a heuristic; its result is the best tour found, not a proof of optimality.</dd>
  <dt>Exploration and exploitation</dt>
  <dd>Exploration tries less-used edges; exploitation returns to trails that already look good. α, β, and ρ shift the balance between them.</dd>
  <dt>Evaporation</dt>
  <dd>The per-round loss of pheromone, written as (1 − ρ) times the current value. It stops old evidence from dominating forever.</dd>
  <dt>Positive feedback</dt>
  <dd>A loop in which a good trail makes the next ant more likely to reuse it, which makes the trail stronger again.</dd>
  <dt>Local optimum</dt>
  <dd>A solution that cannot be improved by a small local change, even though a better solution exists elsewhere.</dd>
  <dt>Diversity (normalized entropy)</dt>
  <dd>A number in [0, 1] describing how evenly pheromone is spread. 1 means every edge has the same share; 0 means all of it is on one edge; a zero total is defined as 1. It is not a probability that the tour is correct.</dd>
</dl>
